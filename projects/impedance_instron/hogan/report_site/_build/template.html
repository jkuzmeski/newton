Controller fit · two objectives

Two controllers.
One shoe.

Two controllers learn to reproduce 98 recorded stances on the slider shoe. The score controller is fitted to every term of the tracking score, including peak force, impulse and contact time. The sample controller is fitted only to the sampled joint coordinates and ground forces. Both are then scored on 9 stances they never saw. Tracking score, lower is better.

One property at a time · measured spring geometry · held-out stances

Stiffness rules.
Energy loss barely matters.

We took the fitted foam and changed one material property at a time, keeping the spring geometry. Both controllers stay frozen: the runner does not re-learn for each foam. Marathon change at coupling 0.65; + means faster.

What moves the marathon.

Bars run from the setting that slows the runner (left) to the one that speeds it up (right). Thin white bars inside are the inner settings (stiffness ×0.7 / ×1.4, slider yield ×0.5). Seconds at coupling 0.65.

Score controllerSample controller

Ranking, the same for both controllers: foam stiffness ≫ hyperfoam exponents > shear-layer fraction ≫ slider yield ≈ no loss > Maxwell share > slider stiffness. The direction agrees between controllers for every property; the sample controller reacts 1.3–3× more strongly. So the honest range for each setting is the span between the two controllers.

Softer helps, until the shoe bottoms out.

Marathon change against foam stiffness scale, with peak column compression under each point. Softer foam lowers the work proxy, but at ×0.5 the columns already compress to 71–76% at peak.

Energy loss sets the press loop, not the run.

On the Instron press (200 ms, 800 N), turning the slider off removes most of the loop: energy loss drops from 11.0% to 2.4%, and to 0% with no loss at all. The runner moves by at most 26 s (dots: orange score, pink sample controller).

Best and worst combinations.

Best = stiffness ×0.5 + exponents ×1.4 + shear layer ×0.5 + no loss. Worst = stiffness ×2 + exponents ×0.7 + shear layer ×2 + slider yield ×2 + slider stiffness ×2 + Maxwell share ×2.

Flag rule (set before the runs): if a variant raises the held-out tracking score more than 25% above the same controller's own original, the runner is out of its fitted range and the work change is not quoted as a speed. Only one result is flagged: the best combination with the score controller (held-out score ×1.375). Its +141 s is not a speed estimate. With the sample controller the best combination stays in range (×1.07) and gives +386 s, barely more than stiffness ×0.5 alone (+375 s): softening beyond ×0.5 buys nothing, because the shoe bottoms out (peak compression 0.72–0.76) and peak force rises 18–20%. The worst combination stays in range for both (×1.21 and ×1.12) and costs 514–748 s.

07Does the recipe transfer?
Second shoe · PUMA Deviate Nitro Elite 3 · same Instron press and last

Same recipe.
Another shoe. 24/24.

Could the foam fit be tuned to FR3? To check, the same fitting process ran unchanged on the Dev3 Instron sweep: same command, bounds, settings and number of starts (7). The FR3-fitted starting points were replaced by Dev3's own earlier fits and by the same quick start generator re-run on Dev3. No FR3 material is used anywhere.

Dev3 held-out conditions pass all three 10% gates (FR3: 24/24)
6/6
repeat cycles at 200 ms pass (hysteresis 2.3%, force 0.8%)

Train and held-out errors agree within 0.05% on both shoes, so no gap between fitted and held-out cycles. An earlier Dev3 fit without the slider passed 9/24.

Loss per cycle falls with load, on both shoes.

Energy lost per press cycle (loop area / loading work), mean over the 4 loading times. Dev3 loses more than FR3 at every load. The Dev3 fit follows its own level and trend within 0.25 points (FR3: within 0.23).

Close material, plausible differences.

Fitted parameters. Same elastic fraction and exponents. Dev3 is 1.24× stiffer, which matches its shorter measured stroke (19.3 vs 21.7 mm at 200 ms, 2800 N). The slider yield, which sets the rate-independent loss, is almost the same.

Two parts do not transfer cleanly.

Shear-layer fraction hits its lower bound (0.01; FR3 fitted 0.072). The bound is active: fixing the fraction costs fit quality (table below). The fraction also trades off with the start offset (2.45 → 1.15 → −1.08 mm). So read it as a fixture and registration correction, not a material constant. Do not carry FR3's 0.072 to other shoes. The slider yield stays at 2.9–3.2 kPa in every variant.
The starting-point recipe is less robust. Only 1 of 7 starts reached the best loss (FR3: 2), and that start stopped at the 80-evaluation cap instead of converging. The winning start was Dev3's own earlier fit. The generated starts stopped 2–4× worse; the best of them is close to the fraction-1.0 row (23/24, hysteresis 2.7%). A shoe without an earlier fit would likely still pass, with about 2.5× the hysteresis error.

Verdict. The fitting process is not overfit to FR3 in fit quality: one Dev3 sample (the only Dev3 sweep) gives 24/24 held-out passes with errors close to FR3's. The fitted shear-layer fraction is shoe- and fixture-specific. No Dev3-specific tuning was done.

08Fine print
What this does not show

Read before
you race.

  • 9 held-out stancesAll results are on 9 stances. 98 more were used for training.
  • One subjectRunner F01 at 3.65 m/s. The 66.5 kg profile is a reconstruction and still needs confirmation.
  • Frozen controllersThe runner does not re-learn for each shoe or foam. A real runner would adapt. Each number is a first-order sensitivity, not a prediction for a runner who re-learns.
  • Two controllers, two answersScore and sample controllers agree in direction everywhere, but not in size. Quote the range.
  • Softer looks betterUnder this work proxy, softer foam always wins until the shoe bottoms out. Comfort, stability and injury are not modelled.
  • Touchdown forceThe simulated impact peak is about 360 N too high, and touchdown comes about 20 ms early.
